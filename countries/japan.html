GF Friendliness

Manageable

Japan's rice-based cuisine offers a strong foundation for gluten-free eating, with sashimi, onigiri, and grilled meats all naturally safe options. The major challenge is soy sauce — it's wheat-based and used in nearly everything from teriyaki to ramen broth — so a translation card explaining your needs and a stash of tamari are essential. Dedicated GF restaurants are growing in Tokyo and Osaka, but outside major cities, careful communication and preparation are key.

Japan-Wide
  • Celiac dining card in Japanese English fluency varies a lot outside major tourist areas, so a printed card explaining celiac disease carries real weight. Worth including the specific kanji restaurants may not think to flag on their own — 小麦 (wheat), plus less obvious ones like 水飴 (mizuame, a syrup that can contain barley or malt) and 澱粉/酵母 (starch/yeast, sometimes questionable). Two hazards that catch American celiacs off guard specifically because they're safe back home: Japanese rice vinegar (su) is often made with barley or malt, unlike most US rice vinegar, and Kewpie mayo — a staple condiment here — contains gluten in Japan even though the US version is GF.

Yes, with real planning. Japan scores 5/10 for GF friendliness overall — Manageable rather than easy. Tokyo and Osaka were the strongest cities on my trip at 7/10 each, with dedicated GF restaurants I could book ahead. Kyoto and Nara came in at 6/10, and Hakone was the toughest at 4/10 — its one dedicated GF café keeps irregular hours and was closed both times I tried to go. Three hidden hazards nationwide: soy sauce (wheat-based, shows up in almost everything), rice vinegar (often made with barley or malt in Japan, unlike most rice vinegar in the US), and Kewpie mayo (contains gluten in the Japanese version, unlike the GF US version). A Japanese celiac card and your own GF soy sauce packets go a long way.
I visited in early March, which meant cooler temperatures throughout (roughly 5–18°C depending on the city) and Kyoto's cherry blossoms just starting to open toward the end of my stay. It also meant real snow in Hakone — heavy enough one day that the Hakone Loop got pushed back and a restaurant closed early. If you want milder weather and don't mind bigger crowds, late March into April (peak cherry blossom season) or October–November (autumn foliage) are the more popular windows — I'd budget extra layers either way if Hakone's mountains are part of your route.
